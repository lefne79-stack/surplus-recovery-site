Part 1

The Nationwide Foreclosure Machine

There are over 3,000 counties in the US, and their government sites are notoriously clunky. Instead of scraping thousands of sites by hand, the machine stacks three APIs to automate the heavy lifting.

1

The Data Engine — Find

Direct access to county recorder data the moment it's filed. Enterprise real estate APIs like ATTOM Data or BatchData aggregate Notice of Default (NOD) and Notice of Trustee Sale (NTS) filings nationwide, usually within 24–48 hours of hitting the courthouse.

2

The Equity Filter — Worth It

A foreclosure lead is useless if the owner owes more than the house is worth. An Automated Valuation Model (AVM) API like HouseCanary compares unpaid loan balance against current market value, filtering out underwater homes so you only see properties with real equity.

3

The Skip-Tracing Layer — Contact

To buy before a public auction, you have to talk to the owner. A skip-tracing API instantly pulls the distressed owner's cell number, email, and known forwarding addresses from the property record.

Part 2

Filing Procedures & Required Documents

Foreclosure isn't a single event — it's a legal timeline. How you buy, and the paperwork you need, changes completely depending on which stage you catch it in.

Stage 1

Pre-Foreclosure — Before the Auction

Triggered when a lender files a Notice of Default (NOD) or Lis Pendens after missed payments. The owner still legally owns the house, so you negotiate directly with them — cash purchase, taking over payments ("Subject-To"), or a bank-approved Short Sale.

Procedure

  • Negotiate directly with the distressed seller
  • Buy for cash, or take over the existing mortgage ("Subject-To")
  • If underwater, pursue a bank-approved Short Sale

Required Documents

  • Purchase and Sale Agreement — the standard contract with the seller
  • Authorization to Release Information — lets you or your title company speak to the lender directly
  • Hardship Letter & Financials — required only for a Short Sale
Stage 2

Public Auction — The Courthouse Steps

Triggered when the lender files a Notice of Trustee Sale (NTS), scheduling an auction. You bid against other investors — often on the courthouse steps or an online portal — for the property entirely "as-is."

Procedure

  • Bid at the courthouse or via an online auction portal
  • Buy as-is, usually without ever entering the property
  • Winning wipes out junior liens but inherits superior liens (e.g. unpaid taxes)

Required Documents

  • Cashier's Checks — no mortgages here; full or partial amount due on the spot
  • Bidding Registration / Proof of Funds — required beforehand for online portals like Hubzu or Auction.com
  • Certificate of Sale — your receipt the moment you win
  • Trustee's / Sheriff's Deed — the recorded transfer of ownership, mailed weeks later
Stage 3

Bank-Owned — REO

If nobody bids high enough at auction, the property reverts to the bank and gets listed with a local agent. Safer than the auction — the bank usually clears the title first, and you can inspect before buying.

Procedure

  • Submit an offer through the bank's listing agent
  • A corporate asset manager reviews the offer
  • Inspection is possible before closing

Required Documents

  • State Purchase Contract (REPC) — your standard local offer paperwork
  • Pre-Approval Letter / Proof of Funds — required before the bank reviews any offer
  • Earnest Money Deposit — 1–3% held in escrow to show you're serious
  • Bank REO Addendum — overrides your state contract and protects the bank on condition disclaimers
The Ultimate Rule

Never buy a property at a Stage 2 auction without running a title search first. If you buy a house without realizing there's a $50,000 IRS tax lien or a city code violation attached, you just bought that debt too.
